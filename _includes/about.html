Sentiment analysis on random movie reviews

Our interest in linguistic phenomena led us to focus on sentiment analysis, specifically analyzing movie reviews, since they offer rich and diverse content, contextual understanding, and varied sentiment expression. Therefore, the task involves categorizing the reviews into positive, negative, or neutral sentiments. We utilized the publicly available IMDB data source, considering the permissible scraping permissions. This dataset comprises individual files containing movie reviews, eliminating the need for extensive preprocessing. Using some Python code, we preprocessed the dataset by removing some HTML tags from the text. Also, we wrote the data in one comprehensive text file, indexing each review in one line. Experiment execution consisted of three sections: dataset collection, annotation, and inter-annotator agreement. The probability of agreement by chance was calculated to be 13.77%. For evaluation, we used inter-annotator agreement and Cohen's Kappa. The observed level of agreement between annotators was measured at 63% and Cohen's Kappa coefficient was computed to be 0.172, indicating a relatively low level of agreement after accounting for chance agreement. In conclusion, while there is a moderate level of agreement observed among us, the annotators, notable discrepancies exist, particularly in categorizing neutral sentiments within the dataset. This experiment emphasizes the necessity for discussions and potential refinement in annotation guidelines to improve the overall agreement among the annotators.

Analyzing similarities in Swadesh's list of words in different languages

In this project we aimed to know if some general words in various languages are similar or if some of the languages in this list borrowed these words from one another. To achieve that, we constructed similar meaning vocabularies across these languages using the Swadesh corpus of the NLTK library. Our languages compromised English, Spanish, French, Italian, Slovak, Romanian, Latin, Catalan, Macedonian, and Swahili. In terms of scoring function, we used a Jaccard similarity-based scoring function, which measures the similarity between two sets by dividing the size of their intersection by the size of their union. In our case, we used this formula considering the vocabularies as sets of words, and for each pair of languages, after calculating the Jaccard similarity between languages using the Swadesh list of words, we made a visualization of the scores as a heatmap. Speaking of the visualization, even though some similar words appear in some pairs of languages, a maximum Jaccard similarity value of 0.07 in the heatmap indicates a low degree of similarity among the sets of words. Conversely, a score of 0.07 suggests that only 7% of the words in the sets overlap, indicating a relatively low level of borrowing among the Swadesh lists in the specified languages. Moreover, A Jaccard similarity from 1% to 7% indicates some word sharing among the languages, such as Spanish and Catalan, Italy and Latin, and French and Catalan having 6% of similar Swadesh list of words on average. While they are not very similar, there are commonalities in the choice of at least some general words across some languages.

Exploring Entropy and Cross-Entropy and Language Modeling

Our goal was to determine the conditional entropy of the word distribution in a text given the previous word. We played around altering the text with mess-up likelihoods from 0% to 10%. Overall, both languages followed similar pattern, having a steady increase when the randomness in the text data rises. The mess of the sample data in each language is the lowest when the likelihood is 10%, while the entropy of the original text results the highest. In terms of baseline entropy when the text was not altered, the uncertainty of English text was 5.287452187472434, while it was 4.747847796480321 in Czech text. As seen in the graph, as we start to increase the mess-up likelihood, the entropy decreases as well indicating that even minor alterations significantly influence the language structure. Speaking of word-level of alterations, the entropy stays considerably stable compared to the character-level alterations. Additionally, since the two languages follows the same pattern regarding the mess-up alterations, both English and Czech languages are sensitive to character changes.
